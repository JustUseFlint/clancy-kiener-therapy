Many couples who come to therapy say the same thing in the first session: *we should have done this sooner.* It is common for partners to live with a painful pattern for a long time before reaching out. Some hope things will improve on their own. Some worry that therapy means the relationship is failing. Others are not sure their problems are "serious enough."

If you are wondering whether it is time, that question itself is worth taking seriously. This guide walks through common signs that couples therapy could help, the myths that hold people back, and what to expect if you decide to begin.

## Ten signs couples therapy could help

Every relationship has hard seasons. These signs suggest a pattern has formed that may be difficult to shift on your own.

### You keep having the same argument

The topic changes, but the fight feels identical. Nothing gets resolved, and both of you leave feeling unheard. Recurring conflict is usually a sign of an underlying pattern rather than a single disagreement.

### Conversations escalate quickly

A small comment becomes a big fight within minutes. Voices rise, old grievances surface, and things are said that hurt. When escalation is the norm, it becomes hard to talk about anything important.

### One of you has stopped bringing things up

Silence can be as concerning as conflict. If one partner has decided that raising issues is not worth the fallout, important needs go unspoken and distance grows quietly.

### You feel more like roommates than partners

You manage the household, the calendar and the kids well, but there is little affection, curiosity or fun. Many couples drift into this without noticing until it feels lonely.

### Trust has been damaged

An affair, a hidden financial decision, a pattern of lying or a broken promise has shaken the foundation. Rebuilding trust is difficult, and guidance can make a real difference.

### A big life change has strained things

A new baby, a move, a job loss, an illness, a death, an empty nest or caring for an aging parent can put enormous pressure on a relationship, even a strong one.

### Intimacy has changed

Physical or emotional intimacy has faded, become a source of tension, or stopped altogether, and talking about it feels awkward or painful.

### You disagree about something big

Children, money, faith, where to live or how to parent. Some disagreements feel impossible to resolve without someone giving up something important.

### Old patterns from your families keep showing up

You notice yourselves repeating dynamics you saw growing up, such as avoidance, criticism or explosive anger, even though you promised you would not.

### You are unsure whether to stay together

Couples therapy is not only for couples who are sure they want to stay. It can also help partners make a clear, respectful decision about the future.

### A note on safety

Couples therapy is designed for relationships where both partners feel safe. If there is physical violence, threats, or one partner feels afraid of the other, joint sessions may not be appropriate at first. In that situation, individual support is usually the safer starting point, and a therapist can help you think through options and resources. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

## Myths that keep couples from starting

### "Therapy is a last resort"

Many people think of couples therapy as something you try right before divorce. In reality, it often works best earlier, before patterns become deeply entrenched and resentment builds. Premarital counseling is a good example of therapy used preventively.

### "The therapist will take sides"

A skilled marriage and family therapist does not have a favorite partner. Their focus is the relationship and the pattern between you. Both people should feel heard, and both should feel gently challenged.

### "It means we failed"

Seeking help is not a sign of failure. It is a sign that you care enough about the relationship to invest in it. Many of the healthiest couples are the ones who are willing to ask for support.

### "We just need to communicate better, and we can do that ourselves"

Sometimes that is true. But when the same communication problems keep coming back despite real effort, an outside perspective can help you see what you cannot see from inside the pattern.

### "Therapy will just be talking about the past"

Some exploration of history can be useful, especially family of origin. But much of couples work is practical and present-focused: what happens between you now, and what you can do differently this week.

## How to bring it up with a hesitant partner

It is very common for one partner to feel more ready for therapy than the other. Some ideas that help:

- **Choose a calm moment,** not the middle of an argument.
- **Lead with the relationship, not the problem.** "I love you and I want us to feel closer. I think some help could make that easier."
- **Avoid framing it as fixing them.** Therapy is about the relationship and the pattern, which involves both people.
- **Acknowledge their concerns.** If they worry about cost, time or being judged, take those concerns seriously.
- **Suggest a trial.** Agreeing to a few sessions feels less daunting than an open-ended commitment.

If your partner is not ready, you can still begin with [individual therapy](services/individual-therapy/). Because relationships are systems, changes in one partner often influence the whole relationship.

## What the first few sessions usually look like

Knowing what to expect can make starting much easier.

### The first session

You will talk together about what is bringing you in, what you have already tried, and what each of you hopes will change. Clancy Kiener describes the first session as a time to discuss what is bringing you to therapy and begin identifying goals that matter to you. Some therapists also meet with each partner briefly on their own.

### Understanding the cycle

Early sessions often focus on mapping the pattern you get caught in: what sets it off, what each person feels and does, and how it usually ends. Many couples feel relief when they see the cycle clearly, because it becomes something to work on together rather than something to blame each other for.

### Practicing something new

As therapy continues, sessions become a place to try different ways of talking and listening, with support in the room. You will often leave with small, specific practices to try at home.

### Spacing out

As things improve, sessions can become less frequent. The aim is for you to handle hard conversations on your own, with tools you trust.

## How to prepare for your first session

You do not need to prepare anything formal, but a little reflection beforehand can help you make the most of your time:

- **Think about what prompted you to reach out now.** Was there a specific argument, event or realization? Naming it helps the therapist understand what feels most urgent.
- **Consider what you want to be different.** Try to describe it in positive terms: "I want us to be able to talk about money without fighting," rather than only "I want the fighting to stop."
- **Notice your own part.** It is natural to focus on what your partner does. It also helps to reflect honestly on what you tend to do when things get hard.
- **Talk logistics together.** Agree on how you will get to sessions, how you will pay, and whether you prefer in person or telehealth, so those details do not become a new source of tension.
- **Expect some discomfort.** Early sessions can stir up strong feelings. That is a normal sign that important things are being discussed, not a sign that therapy is making things worse.

Most of all, try to come with curiosity. The goal is not to prove who is right, but to understand what is happening between you and what might help.

## The best time to start

There is rarely a perfect time to begin couples therapy. Schedules are busy, finances are tight, and it is easy to wait for things to get worse, or better, before deciding. But patterns tend to deepen with time, and resentment grows when needs go unmet.
