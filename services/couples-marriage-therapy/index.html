Understanding couples and marriage therapy

Most couples do not come to therapy because they have stopped caring. They come because they care a great deal and keep ending up in the same argument, the same silence or the same lonely evening. Couples and marriage therapy gives that pattern a place to be seen clearly, by both of you, with a licensed marriage and family therapist who is not on either side.

Clancy Kiener is a Licensed Marriage and Family Therapist with more than fifteen years in practice. Her work centers on authentic communication: helping each partner say what is actually true for them, and helping the other partner hear it without bracing for a fight. Sessions are practical and warm. You will leave with a clearer picture of what is happening between you, and with specific ways to respond differently at home.

Because her training is rooted in family systems, Clancy also looks at what each of you brought into the relationship: the family you grew up in, the way conflict was handled there, and the expectations you may not know you carry. Understanding that history often takes the blame out of the room and replaces it with curiosity.

Who it is for

  • You argue about the same things and nothing gets resolved
  • You feel more like roommates or co-parents than partners
  • Trust has been hurt and you both want to try to repair it
  • A big transition (a new baby, a move, a loss, an empty nest) has strained the relationship
  • One of you shuts down while the other pursues
  • You are not sure whether to stay together and want a careful place to decide

What sessions look like

The shape of the work

  1. Your first session

    You will talk together about what is bringing you in, what has already been tried, and what each of you hopes will be different. Clancy will ask about the history of the relationship and begin to name goals that matter to both of you.

  2. Seeing the pattern

    Over the next sessions the focus moves to the cycle you get caught in: what sets it off, what each of you feels and does, and how it usually ends. Naming the cycle together is often the first real relief.

  3. Practicing something new

    Sessions become a place to try a different kind of conversation while support is in the room. You will take small, concrete practices home between sessions.

  4. Consolidating

    As things settle, sessions can space out. The aim is for you to be able to handle hard conversations on your own, with tools you trust.

Benefits

What can change

Clearer communication

Learn to say the real thing, not the angry version of it, and to listen without preparing a defense.

Less escalation

Spot the moment an argument turns and use simple ways to pause and come back.

Repair after hurt

Work through betrayals and old injuries at a pace that is honest for both partners.

Renewed closeness

Make room again for affection, humor and the friendship underneath the conflict.

Shared direction

Get on the same page about money, family, faith, parenting and the future.

A neutral guide

A therapist who keeps the conversation fair, safe and focused on the relationship.

Approach & modalities

How Clancy works

Clancy draws on Family / Marital and Family Systems therapy, Interpersonal and Object Relations perspectives, and Solution Focused Brief Therapy. She often uses Cognitive Behavioral Therapy to look at how the stories each partner tells about the other shape what they feel and do.
