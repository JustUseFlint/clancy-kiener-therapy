What to Expect in Your First Therapy Session in Nashville

A calm, step-by-step guide to starting therapy: how to book, what happens in the first session, questions you might be asked, questions to ask your therapist, cost, telehealth and how to know it is a good fit.

Key takeaways

  • Feeling nervous before a first session is completely normal
  • The first session is mostly about getting to know you and what you want to change
  • You can share as much or as little as you are ready to
  • Asking your therapist questions is encouraged and helps you judge fit
  • Clear information about fees, scheduling and privacy should be available up front

Deciding to start therapy is a meaningful step. For many people it comes after weeks, months or even years of thinking about it. And yet, even after the decision is made, the first session can feel intimidating. What will they ask? What should I say? What if I cry, or what if I don't know where to start?

If you are feeling nervous, you are in good company. Almost everyone does. This guide walks through what typically happens before, during and after a first therapy session, so that you can walk in, or log on, feeling a little more prepared.

Before your first session

Reaching out

The first step is usually the hardest: making contact. With Clancy Kiener at Quiet Mind Therapy, you can reach out in whichever way feels most comfortable:

  • Fill out the short, private Get Started form on this site, which prepares a note you can send by email or text
  • Call or text 615-423-4140
  • Email clancy@quietmindtherapy.com
  • Book directly through the online scheduler

When you schedule an appointment, a reminder is sent by text, email or both. There is no pressure to schedule before you feel ready. As the practice puts it, take your time, and schedule when it feels right.

Practical details

Before your first session, it helps to know the basics:

  • Format: sessions are available in person at 4235 Hillsboro Pike in Nashville, or by telehealth.
  • Timing: appointments are scheduled Monday through Saturday, most hours.
  • Length: individual sessions are listed at 55 minutes.
  • Cost: individual and couple sessions are $150. Premarital counseling is $400 for a two-hour session.
  • Insurance: Clancy does not take insurance, but accepts HSA cards and many other forms of payment. A sliding scale may be available depending on current availability and circumstances.

You also have the right to ask for a Good Faith Estimate of what your care is expected to cost.

Paperwork

Most therapists ask you to review and sign some forms before or at the start of the first session. These typically cover confidentiality and its limits, practice policies such as cancellations, and consent to treatment. It is worth reading them carefully and asking about anything that is unclear.

During the first session

Getting settled

Whether you are meeting in an office or on a screen, the first few minutes are usually about getting comfortable. For telehealth, find a private space where you will not be overheard, with a reliable connection. Headphones can help with privacy.

Talking about confidentiality

Early in the first session, your therapist will usually explain confidentiality. What you share is kept private, with a small number of legal exceptions, such as imminent danger to yourself or others, suspected abuse or neglect, or a court order. Knowing these boundaries up front helps you decide what to share.

Exploring what brings you in

Most of the first session is spent getting to know you and understanding why you are seeking therapy now. Clancy describes the first session this way: you will discuss what is bringing you to therapy and begin identifying goals that matter to you.

You might be asked questions such as:

  • What made you decide to reach out now?
  • How long has this been going on?
  • How is it affecting your daily life, sleep, work or relationships?
  • What have you already tried?
  • Who are the important people in your life?
  • What was your family like growing up?
  • What are you hoping will be different?

You do not need perfect answers. "I don't know" is a perfectly good answer. You also get to decide what to share and when. It is fine to say, "I'm not ready to talk about that yet."

You don't have to have the words yet. Finding them is part of the work.

Starting to think about goals

By the end of a first session, many people have a beginning sense of what they want to work on. Goals might be specific, such as sleeping better or arguing less with a partner, or broader, such as feeling more like yourself. Goals can change as therapy continues.

Approach and modalities

Your therapist may also share a little about how they work. Clancy often uses Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), which helps explore how thoughts shape emotions and everyday experience, and draws on other approaches including family systems, person-centered, narrative and solution-focused therapy.

How the session ends

Toward the end of the first session, your therapist will usually summarize what they heard, check whether it fits your experience, and talk with you about next steps. This is a good moment to share how the session felt, ask about anything that was unclear, and decide together when to meet again. Some people leave with a small reflection or practice to try before the next appointment.

Questions you can ask your therapist

A first session is also your opportunity to decide whether this therapist is a good fit for you. It is completely appropriate to ask questions such as:

  • What is your experience with the kind of issue I'm facing?
  • How do you usually work with clients like me?
  • What does a typical session look like?
  • How often do you recommend meeting?
  • How will we know if therapy is helping?
  • What happens if I need to cancel?

A good therapist will welcome these questions. Clancy states it plainly: if she is not the right fit, she will work with you to find a fit that better suits your needs.

Common worries, answered

"What if I cry?" Tears are a normal part of therapy, and therapists are comfortable with them. There is no need to apologize. Crying often means something important is being touched.

"What if I don't know what to talk about?" That is very common. Your therapist will guide the conversation with questions, and it is fine to start with whatever is on your mind that day, even if it seems small.

"What if my problem isn't serious enough?" You do not need a crisis to deserve support. Many people come to therapy because they feel stuck, stressed or simply want to understand themselves better.

"Will I be judged?" A therapist's role is to understand, not to judge. Therapists hear a wide range of experiences, and what feels shameful to you is often something they have helped many others with.

"What if I need to stop?" You are always free to pause or end therapy. Ideally, you would talk it through with your therapist first, so you can reflect on what has helped and plan next steps.

After your first session

Feelings afterward

It is common to feel a mix of things after a first session: relief, tiredness, vulnerability, hope, or even a little stirred up. Talking about difficult things can bring feelings closer to the surface. Be gentle with yourself for the rest of the day. A walk, a quiet evening or time with someone supportive can help.

Deciding on next steps

Most people schedule regular sessions at first, then adjust frequency as things improve. You and your therapist can decide this together. If something about the session did not feel right, it is worth naming it at the start of the next session. Good therapy relationships can handle honest feedback.

Consistency matters

Therapy tends to be most effective when sessions are attended consistently. Quiet Mind Therapy's policy is that three cancellations or missed appointments within 24 hours of a session will result in a referral to another provider. Knowing this in advance helps you plan.

Individual, couples or family?

If you are not sure which type of therapy fits, here is a simple guide:

  • Individual therapy focuses on you: your thoughts, feelings, history and goals.
  • Couples and marriage therapy focuses on the relationship between two partners.
  • Premarital counseling helps engaged couples prepare for marriage.
  • Family therapy looks at patterns across a family.
  • Focused support is available for anxiety, addiction and trauma and family-of-origin healing.

If you are still unsure, describe what is going on when you reach out, and Clancy can help you decide.

What therapy is not

Therapy is a powerful tool, but it is not an emergency service. If you are in crisis or worried about your safety, call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, or call 911. It is also worth knowing what falls outside a therapist's role. For example, Clancy does not complete ADA or FMLA paperwork, provide Emotional Support Animal letters, or provide court testimony unless she receives a court order. Knowing this up front avoids surprises.

Taking the first step

Starting therapy is an act of courage and care. You do not need to have everything figured out, and you do not need to arrive in crisis. You only need to be willing to begin.

When you are ready, you can fill out the short Get Started form, call or text 615-423-4140, or book online. Clancy sees clients in person in Nashville and by telehealth, Monday through Saturday.
